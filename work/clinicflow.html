ClinicFlow — booking in 41 seconds.

A three-clinic healthcare group's booking flow took four screens and a phone call. Patients gave up; the front desk became a call center. We redesigned the flow until it respected the patient's busyness.

The problem

The form asked everything. The patient owed almost none of it.

Watching session recordings, the drop pattern was blunt: a seven-field form demanded date of birth, address, insurance ID, referring doctor, and reason-for-visit before it would show a single available slot. Sixty-one percent of patients abandoned at that wall, and the ones who wanted the appointment anyway called — which is the most expensive booking channel a clinic can run.

We were gating the calendar behind an intake form. Nobody books a haircut that way — why a clinic? — Clinic director
Constraints & decisions

Slots first. Everything else can wait.

Availability before information→ Patients pick a service, see open slots, and hold one for 10 minutes before any personal data. The wall moved to after the win.
Four fields, pre-filled where legal→ Name, phone, and one identifier. Address and insurance collected at check-in — where staff verify it anyway.
Calendar as the home screen→ The month grid leads; density per day, six free slots flagged in ember. Scanning beats filtering for a 3-service clinic.
Tested with real patients, not staff→ Five rounds with patients aged 24–71. The 41-second median and the 92% task success came from them, not from us.
What shipped

A design system the clinic can actually maintain.

Deliverables went beyond screens: a 31-token design system (type, color, spacing, focus states), component specs with every interaction state, an accessibility annotation layer for the dev team, and the prototype used for patient testing. Implementation docs were written so the clinic's existing developers could ship it without guessing — and they did, in three weeks.

Outcome & the honest retro

The front desk got its afternoons back.

Within two months, booking calls dropped 53% and no-shows fell as SMS reminders landed in the same flow. The design system outlived the project — the clinic's patient portal adopted it, which is the sincerest form of flattery a design team can receive.

What I'd do differently: I'd fight harder against one field — "reason for visit." Clinical staff wanted it for prep; patients skipped it 40% of the time anyway. A compromise (optional, last step) tested better and I should have proposed it in round one, not round three.

Finally a developer who explains trade-offs in plain language — and then delivers exactly that.
